## Introduction
Quantum computers were first proposed by Richard Feynman and Paul Benioff in 1981, designed to exploit the intrinsic quantum properties of quantum physics in order to achieve computational gains potentially orders of magnitudes greater than their classical counterparts. Though initially conceived for scientific purposes – simulating the evolution of a quantum system (a task impossible for a classical computer to accurately perform), quantum computers have an unrealised potential in a large variety of other fields. Many large corporations and well-funded start-ups have emerged in order to try exploit this, from Google and Microsoft to IBM. Recently, quantum computing has often been portrayed within the media as the next big leap in computing, potentially ushering in a new technological era just as their classical counterparts did in the 1990’s. However, these claims have been consistently voiced across the past decade, with seemingly little actual progress towards any consumer facing product. 
 
This paper aims to understand to the transformative ability of quantum computing as both a viable alternative to classical computation, as well as the potential for quantum computing to open up new possibilities beyond the realm of classical computers. In order to be classed as a viable alternative in any one field, the author seeks to determine whether quantum computers have any practical benefit over pre-existing classical computers, whether this benefit is outweighed by other mitigating factors such as running costs, and whether the technology is likely to be successfully developed and implemented within the next decade. However, determining the potential for quantum computation to conduct novel tasks, is considerably more challenging – just as few early developers of the computer might have predicted the online revolution of the computer-enabled internet. Whilst the future is inherently difficult to accurately determine, by first analysing the fundamental building blocks of quantum computers, this paper aims to understand where the theoretical difficulties will lie within any future quantum computer, given the assumption that the most fundamental operating principles of quantum computer construction shall remain unchanged. Furthermore, by analysing the fundamentals of quantum computation, it is also possible to understand where and why quantum computers might have any chance of superseding classical computation, known as quantum supremacy. However, this paper does not discuss quantum supremacy itself, which is a highly theoretical benchmark between the most powerful supercomputers and the most powerful quantum computers, but rather looks to discuss possible practical supremacy, which though harder to quantitively gauge is the key determining factor in adoption of quantum computers. 
 
The discussion may be decomposed into two key sections:
1.     The fundamental mechanics behind quantum computer operations, and an analysis of the resulting benefits and limitations.
2.     The possible viable applications of quantum computing, and how they compare taking the classical computers used for each task as qualitative benchmarks.
It is only by appreciating both where any quantum gains emerge from, and what the quantum benefits can conceivably be used on, can we hope to discern the future of quantum computation.

This paper has the stated aim of providing an introduction into quantum computing for non-physicists. Though some level of technical background is assumed, with a required proficiency in linear algebra to progress through certain sections, overall, the paper achieves its aim. The paper initially discusses qubits (quantum equivalent of binary bits), providing a mathematical basis for them through the use of ket notation of vector-states. The authors gradually widen in scope to provide explanation of the implications of quantum entanglement and superposition on multiple qubits, as well as providing insight into the problem of measurement. Whilst entangling states into qubits is simple, the paper demonstrates how we can only get one state out of a quantum computer, reducing its potential given the large number of states we can program into it through superposition. The authors give a demonstration of how probabilities are used to skew the likelihood of a useful outcome in our favour. Being a paper targeted at non-physicists, the authors provide concise explanations of the underlying quantum phenomena which are relevant to quantum computers, however these explanations often require the layman reader to assume many facts as a given, giving a shallower depth of understanding on the underlying physics. The paper later provides the reader with insight into how a quantum computer could work, giving a mathematical basis for quantum transformation through linear matrix transformations, which act as quantum logic gates. The notation for these logic gates are also introduced, as well as some example gates like the Cnot gate and the Hadamard transformation. The paper then continues by describing quantum algorithms like Shor’s factor algorithm, which hold little relevance to this paper.
 
Overall, the paper has proven most useful as a source of understanding as to where the massive computational benefits of quantum computing are derived from. Properties of quantum computers like quantum parallelism and the linear scaling of otherwise exponential processor requirement increases due to a superposition of output states are given with the nuance of their drawbacks, for example, the limitations surrounding measurement of a quantum system. However, the paper only describes how quantum computers work on a theoretical level, as opposed to analysing the technical limitations and viability issues that might occur when constructing one, which reduces the paper’s utility. This paper has one major drawback, having been written 23 years ago in September of 2000. Whilst the paper is peer-reviewed, written by reputable authors, and has received a total 178 citations, the study’s age is incongruous with the modern quantum computing landscape given what has been a rapidly evolving field over the past 20 years. In spite of this, the fundamental building blocks of how quantum computers operate have changed little, and, when corroborated with excerpts of more recent papers, as a description of how quantum computers work, the paper’s information is still largely valid. It is mainly the solutions to the limitations listed in the paper that have evolved, so it follows that descriptions of how quantum computers operate, in terms of the underlying physics, must still be accurate, given the laws of physics have continued to operate as they did 23 years ago. Unfortunately, this does have the implication that the paper’s details on how quantum computers are constructed are outdated and thus unusable, though, given the paper seeks only to describe a quantum computer at a theoretical level, rather than an applied, technical one, the more abstract and theoretical facets of the paper do stand the test of time.

## Quantum parallelism
The ability of a system multiple qubits to exist in a large number of states simultaneously through superposition has powerful implications. Applying a function to the superposition of states will transform all the superposed states simultaneously at no extra computing cost. This property of quantum computers is known as quantum parallelism, and, when exploited correctly, serves as a key aspect of any theorised quantum supremacy. 
 
However, there exists a major caveat, though quantum computers can simulate multiple states simultaneously, they can output only the result of one of those states. This is derived from the property of measurement that renders it physically impossible to recover the initial superposed states post-collapse, resulting in only the one collapsed output. ## Application of classical algorithms on quantum circuitry.
Quantum logic gates, by virtue of their underlying unitary transformations, are always reversible, unlike many key classical gates. However, quantum computers can still perform any classical computational algorithm despite having access only to reversible gates. For example, the Toffoli gate can perform all irreversible Boolean operators. 
Crucially, though quantum computers can perform any classical algorithm, they are unable to do so any more efficiently or reliably than classical computers can. Thus, achieving a quantum advantage is not simply a case of applying pre-existing classical algorithms to a faster computer, but rather a paradigm shift in the computation itself. 
## Summary
In order to achieve quantum supremacy, any quantum algorithm must rely upon the quantum phenomena, such as superposition and entanglement, that underpin how quantum computers function, including taking full advantage of the emergent properties such as quantum parallelism and exponential scaling that set quantum computers apart from their classical alternatives. As a result of this, it is the author’s belief that quantum computers are unlikely to replace classical computers, but rather carve their own niche in regions that classical computers cannot currently venture. 
 
## Challenges of constructing quantum computers
There are a multitude of barriers inhibiting the development of quantum computers which must be overcome before quantum computers can viably hope to challenge classical computation.
 
## Decoherence
The most prominent of issues plaguing quantum computation is the problem of decoherence, with quantum computers susceptible to decoherence generally described as “noisy”. States of superposition are highly fragile and are susceptible to losing their coherent properties when interacting with the environment. This results in the premature collapse of any superposition and leakage of information to the environment, which physically cannot ever be recovered, limiting the time that a quantum computer can functionally perform operations on qubits. Furthermore, decoherence results in a reduction in qubit scaling efficiency, for the necessary pre-emption of decoherence and resulting assumption that not all qubits will remain functional produces quantum computers designed with a greater number of qubits than necessarily required. It is not currently the case that the main difficulty in constructing quantum computers lies in increasing the qubit count, but rather in increasing the coherent qubit count. Therefore, in order to combat this issue, quantum computers must be kept as near to absolute zero as possible. At 0.015 Kelvin, a quantum computer is the coldest known place in the universe, thus simply maintaining such temperatures requires vast level of complexity and energy.
 
## Error correction
Efforts in combating system decoherence have largely relied not upon hardware improvements but rather efficient error correction protocols and other similar mitigation techniques. Unlike classical computers, which have discrete errors, the variable probabilistic nature of quantum computing result in continuous errors, which involve both amplitude and phase. Furthermore, the inability to copy quantum states, as result of the no-cloning principle, and the property of measurement that collapses quantum states, destroying superposed information in the process, means that highly developed and efficient classical error correction algorithms cannot be applied to their quantum counterparts - novel methods of correction must be sourced.
 
## Utility of measurement
Once the quantum state of a system has been measured, it is impossible to determine the initial superposed states. Furthermore, due to the no cloning principle, a qubit’s state cannot be copied and measured multiple times to unveil more information. This greatly reduces the theoretical advantage that quantum computers may have, greatly restricting the power of quantum parallelism. Thus, in order to ensure measurements are useful, the transformations of qubits through logic gates and circuitry must influence the probabilities in such a way that the desired solutions are outputted, which further limits the range and increases the complexity of quantum algorithms.

## Type 1 applications: Direct benefits of quantum computation
Type 1 applications are applications resulting directly from the superior ability of quantum computers to perform certain tasks. These stem from the two main quantum computational benefits: quantum scaling and quantum parallelism. Quantum computers are, at a fundamental level, inherently more capable than their classical counterparts at three main types of tasks:
1.     Combinatorics problems 
2.     Searching and sorting algorithms
3.     Exact models
 
## Combinatorics problems
The aim of combinatorics problems is to figure out the number of possible combinations, or the best possible combinations in some metric of a set of options. An example of this would be the travelling salesman problem, where a salesman needs to visit n number of cities in as short a distance as possible, in one loop. Though initially trivial for a classical computer, the complexity of such problems grows exponentially as n increases. Therefore, for large problems analogous to the traveling salesman, room sized classical supercomputers are required. Quantum computers however are capable of processing large volumes of data at the same time through quantum parallelism, permitting them to solve large and highly complex problems with the collapse of a superposition.
 
## Searching and sorting algorithms
The ability to rapidly search and sort large volumes of data is fundamental to any computational algorithm, from machine learning to international supply chains. ## Exact models
The original conception of quantum computation by Richard Feynman in 1981 was as a means to simulate nature exactly. Whilst classical computers must necessarily reduce any complex simulation down to discrete binary bits, which result in approximations and the omission of fine details, leading to the propagation of errors, quantum computers, by virtue of their inherent continuous quantum nature, are theoretically able to model nature exactly. This has the potential to result in many major advancements in a multitude­­ of fields, and also is the root source of many Type-2 applications’ benefits. In order to generate exact models capable of simulating quantum phenomena beyond the reach of classical computers, small scale systems ranging from only (50-100) coherent qubits are required, known as quantum simulators. The author views these simulators as a highly viable stepping stone for the development of quantum computers, allowing quantum computation to have a large impact at a relatively early stage of their development.
 
## Type 2 applications: Quantum enabled classical technologies.
Quantum enabled classical technologies are algorithms and process designed around classical computers that have been adapted for and enhanced by quantum computers. These applications utilise quantum computers in conjunction to, or as a replacement for classical computers. 
 
## Quantum biochemical engineering: High Throughput Screening
Currently, drug development is a long, drawn-out process of trial and error, requiring billions of dollars in expenditure, and often taking over a decade for a single drug. As such, un-subsidised drug costs are high, and many potentially curable diseases deprioritised in favour of curing higher reward diseases. Thus, if quantum computers can disrupt this industry and improve both drug efficacy and streamline the research process, there is a potential to save a significant number of lives. However, in order to analyse any potential benefit quantum computers may provide to the pharmaceutical industry, it is important to first understand where the time and costs are most heavily invested.
 
The current most common method of drug discovery is high-throughput screening, where a large molecular library is screened through in vitro experiments. These typically follow a 5-stage process:
1.     Generate library of potential drug candidates.
2.     Screen based on medicinal activity to identify chemicals knowns as hits that exhibit desired medicinal activity against a disease.
3.     Screen further based on factors like efficacy and potency of hits.
4.     Optimise into candidates called lead compounds.
5.     Proceed through stages of drug development and clinical trials till one lead becomes a viable commercial product.
Of this process, a significant portion of time and money is invested in manufacturing and identifying hits, which requires the synthesis of a large number of compounds. Whilst classical computers, introduced in the 1970s, have increased hit rates and lead quality, they can only accurately simulate molecules of low molecular weight (less than 900 Daltons), resulting in 78% of the current drugs market being of low molecular weight.
 
By simulating the ability of a potential drug to bind to a biological target, computers can streamline the discovery process, however, quantum computers, through quantum parallelism and exact models, are able to more accurately and rapidly simulate the complex chemistry required to screen possible hits.
 
## Numerical weather prediction
Weather simulations models are highly complex, with the chaotic nature of weather patterns being a significant challenge to classical computers. Problems such as the most likely path of a tropical cyclone require vast, power hungry, classical supercomputers to find near-accurate approximate solutions. As a result, current weather prediction models, though reasonably accurate, are limited by the inherent discrete nature of classical bits, for any set of continuous data must be reduced to a binary ones and zeros. Furthermore, the computational capacity of classical bits is far too minimal to produce sufficiently accurate predictions into the future. However, quantum computers do not face similar limitations, for qubits are inherently continuous, which allows quantum computers to take less approximations than classical computers, and the quantum scaling of quantum computers, combined with the power of quantum parallelism allows quantum computers to perform more powerful simulations. Though these improvements are rooted in the ability of quantum computers to perform exact models, it should be noted that quantum computers will not be able to simulate weather exactly, rather they shall provide dramatic gains in reliability and prediction length.
 
The possible impacts of improved numerical weather predictions are vast, for with improved weather prediction capabilities comes the ability to evacuate and warn people in the path of storms. Furthermore, improvements to weather models can help simulate flood surges, which allows governments to better prepare in advance with reinforced embankments and higher densities of cyclone or tornado shelters.

## Quantum internet
A quantum internet utilises quantum phenomena to send information, stored in qubits, over long distances with absolute security. The security of a quantum internet relies upon a technique known as Quantum Key Distribution. If one party, sends an entangled qubit, retaining the other of the pair, to another party, who, upon receiving the qubit, measures it, the measurements of both parties can be freely compared over a public channel without any threat to the encoded data, as they do not individually reveal the original message  (Manojlovic, 2017). This also means, if they do not match, that the entanglement has collapsed, and someone has intruded. But as the individual decohered qubits are of no help, the interceptor cannot decipher any viable message (Ananthaswamy, 2019).
 

![[unknown 70.png|unknown 70.png]]

Figure 11 - Diagram of a quantum internet.
The network is comprised of quantum computer end-nodes. The switch directs single qubits along the system which get sent to a quantum repeater, which act as a range extender for the entangled qubit, as sending qubits over long distances will increase the chance of losing information to decoherence (Wehner, 2018). A benefit of quantum the internet is that wires used do not have to be re-laid, for entangled photons, used as qubits, can be sent down existing optical fibres with little loss of reliability. This allows an easier transition to a quantum internet, using the existing infrastructure.
 
However, quantum error correction schemes capable of protecting qubits would require a larger number of qubits, and so, a larger quantum computer capable of coding and decoding the information. Redundancy, contrary to classical bits, cannot be built into a quantum system due to the no cloning theorem. There are, however, some codes that can protect against redundant qubits, for example Shor code. These work by distributing information across multiple qubits so operation errors and loss errors can be corrected (QuTech).
Quantum internets have yet to leave the laboratory but scaled tests have occurred. There have also been successful networks in Geneva (CERN to Geneva university), a setup in Tokyo in partnership with the EU, which, for the first time, implemented a higher-data rate encryption capable of supporting secure voice and video conferencing.

## Conclusion
This paper concludes that quantum computing is a rapidly developing field, one in which sustained growth is near guaranteed. Quantum computers have incredible potential, with powerful new computational methods like quantum parallelism, resulting from entanglement and the collapse of superpositions. Furthermore, quantum scaling allows quantum computers to scale exponentially in computational ability with a linear increase in qubits, resulting from the “extra” entangled states that classical computers, by means of their discrete bits, cannot use. When combined into larger algorithms, such as Grover’s search algorithm, these benefits permit any algorithm designed for quantum computers to outperform any equivalent classical computer. 
 
However, it is the practical impact of the upcoming introduction of quantum computation within the next decade that shall determine its status as a transformative technology.
