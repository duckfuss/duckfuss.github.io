# Introduction
----
This essay will be discussing the viability of establishing Mars colony within the coming 20 years, from 2022 to 2042. This involves looking primarily at the technological challenges of setting foot and establishing a colony on Mars, whether the practical constraints of budget and public support are likely to be a limiting factor as well as whether Mars travel is something the human race can benefit from and should pursue. Mars has long been seen as a milestone for the human race's ever-growing drive towards expansion, making us an inter-planetary species, and going there in person would have huge benefits towards science, for Martian rovers and landers cannot do everything humans are capable of. Mars has also captured the public imagination ever since 1877, when Giovanni Schiaparelli observed, during a favourable conjunction, what he described as "canali", Italian for naturally occurring channel. This was mistranslated into English as canals and sparked huge interest into the possibility of life on Mars. Now Mars has reason to come yet again into the forefront of the public consciousness, for, according to NASA, humans may land on Mars by the mid 2030's. However, a trip to Mars, especially within the next two decades, has a high financial cost, and would require significant political capital, the viability of which, in a world plagued by conflict and rife with inequality is by no means a certainty.  

# Methodology

## The potential for Bias

Initially, the author has approached this project with the opinion that going to Mars would be a good step for humanity. This view has inevitably changed as more research has been conducted deeper into the topic, and, whilst the author has attempted to remain as impartial as possible, some element of bias of the author’s opinion undoubtedly has come across.

## The focus on NASA

For the technical evaluation of getting to Mars, this report largely focuses on NASA’s plans to arrive there. This is because NASA is the only space agency that already has detailed plans, experience, and prior investment (in the form of Artemis) to make journey to Mars and is thus seen as the most viable route in which humanity could get to Mars. Nevertheless, the challenges faced by NASA will be similar to those faced by any other agency aiming to reach Mars, as will the ethical and moral questions raised.

This report has aimed to use as varied, unbiased, and truthful sources as possible.

## Challenges of establishing a colony on Mars

### The risk of radiation

The risk of radiation increases substantially during solar storms, in more active periods of the sun's solar cycle. And is forever present on the surface of Mars itself (Fig.1)

Astronauts also need to be protected from Galactic Cosmic Rays (GCR's) which are particles accelerated to near light speed from other stars in the Milky Way or even other galaxies. This form of radiation is more ionising, and a lot more difficult to shield against. (Frazier, 2015). The problems don't stop there though, Mars itself has 50 times more radiation exposure than the Earth, at around 240-300 mSv per year, which contrasts with Earth's 6.2 mSv per year, and the International Commission on Radiation Protection's recommended max limit for radiation worker's 50 mSv per year. (Marspedia.org, 2021) This is because Mars does not have a magnetosphere, a magnetic field that Earth has, due to its molten iron core, that repels the radiation, acting as a shield.

According to NASA, there are two main methods of shielding astronauts, whilst in deep space, from radiation, which is to either add more material around the structure of the spaceship such that it could absorb the ionising particles, or to use more efficient shielding materials at repelling radiation. The first method, though simplest and easier to implement, would be prohibitively expensive, as more mass means more rocket fuel required to launch. With the second solution, in order to successfully block radiation, comprising of protons and neutrons, a similarly sized object is required for the deflection. Hydrogen, with just a single proton and electron blocks both protons and neutrons extremely successfully. Compounds involving hydrogen like water and plastics like polyethylene are prime candidates for deep space shielding, as engineers could make the protection from the already-required mass of astronauts' trash and water. in order to prevent astronauts from over exposure to radiation whilst living on Mars, the techniques listed above can be used in conjunction with piling frozen ![](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image007.png), harvested directly from the atmosphere over the habitat, as-well as covering the habitat in a layer of protective dirt. (JPL, NASA, 2002) Other proposals (Fig. 2) have suggested adding spacecraft in L1 around Mars with 1-2 tesla magnetic fields, acting as a shield, whilst this does sound fanciful and unviable, there is a growing body of research to hint at its possible effectiveness. (Green, et al., 2017)

### Atmosphere of Mars

When astronauts first arrive on the Martian surface, the first thing they will have to contend with is the relatively unsafe atmosphere. Thanks to the curiosity rover, we know that it is 95.2% ![](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image007.png) with traces of nitrogen, argon, oxygen, and carbon monoxide. Technology can be developed to harvest these elements but will require large amounts of energy in order to sustain a colony, and, as with all life support systems, cannot, under any circumstance, go wrong. The Martian atmosphere is also prone to planet-wide, weeklong dust storms capable of blocking out the sun, a big potential worry for prospective astronauts. This phenomenon is still largely a mystery to science, meaning it cannot currently be predicted, a major concern for any future Mars colony, so research and work should be done on this front before any human steps foot on Mars.  
Furthermore, the temperature on Mars, on average is -62℃, compared with Earth's average of 16℃, in fact, the hottest Mars gets is 21℃, as opposed to Earth's 56℃, and the coldest the temperature has been known to drop to has been -128℃, with the Earth's minimum at -88℃. (Sharp & Gordon, 2022) This will severely limit astronaut's time outside, as well as adding the extra requirement of heating, which in turn adds to the payload weight and power needs. Besides adding heating an insulation to a colony, long term solutions of adding greenhouse gasses into the Martian atmosphere to trap its heat have been suggested. This would involve building factories that release artificial greenhouse gasses or melting the ![](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image007.png) trapped in the polar ice caps, potentially with controlled nuclear explosions. But this technology is far from mature, and playing God with the Martian atmosphere has long been seen as a dangerous way of approaching the problem (Fandom, n.d.)

### The Armstrong limit

By far the deadliest concern for astronauts will be the atmospheric pressure. At 0.6% as dense as Earth's averaging at 600 pascals of pressure, compared with Earth's 101.3 kilopascals of atmospheric pressure, water, which, like all substances, has its melting and boiling point affected by pressure, will boil below 36.5℃, the normal human body temperature. In fact, this limit, known as the Armstrong limit, occurs below 6.3 kilopascals, so Mars, which has, from the bottom of the canyon Hellas Planitia, a maximum pressure of 1.16 kilopascals, is far below this dangerous limit. (Vox, 2019) Any exposed humans on Mars would have their bodily fluids, like blood, tears, and the liquids wetting alveoli boil. Therefore, it is important that any human traveling on Mars should remain in a pressurised environment, which must never fail. (Biology-Forums, 2016)

### The Human problem

Humans surviving on Mars is not just about technical concerns to do with Mars and space, how humans interact and handle the mission is as, if not more, important. After-all, the same group of humans will be sharing the same confined space for 1 to 3 years depending on mission scenario. These potential problems can be broken down into two key groups, the health aspects, and the interaction aspects. Maintaining a healthy lifestyle during a major Mars mission is by no means easy. The different gravitational levels affect the cardiovascular system, causing, upon re-entry into a gravity environment, a condition known as orthostatic intolerance, as well as causing increased heart electrical instability. Furthermore, the skeletal muscles in low gravity undergo disuse atrophy, whereby, due to lack of use, they waste away. The skeletal system itself also is greatly affected by exposure to low gravity, where the lack of weight causes bone atrophy, something that does not appear, based on current space missions, to ever recover completely, making this factor one of the most critical for a long spaceflight. There are more issues and challenges to health, NASA having identified 32 key risks, 232 gaps in knowledge and 1097 tasks to complete in order to fulfil the knowledge gaps. (Linck, et al., 2019) Not all personal risks are strictly physical, the 5 main hazards, according to NASA, are altered gravity, radiation, hostile environment (habitability factors like temperature, pressure, lighting, noise, quantity of space), isolation and confinement (which can lead to sleep loss and circadian desynchronisation), and the distance to Earth. (NASA, 2021) These are all huge concerns for any prospective astronaut, and further research is being conducted into the best ways to mitigate these, and other effects. (Linck, et al., 2019)In the future, creating artificial gravity may be a solution, but in the near-term, physical exercise, a healthy diet, and key supplements are vital. Interaction and crew relations are also imperative to any successful mission. Research in this area is based on extensive analogues, like the biosphere experiments. A Mars mission will create a condition known as the artificial society, whereby a group is living and working in conditions being reliant on technology like life-support. Within this condition, life requires strong adaptability, characteristics of these situations being isolation, confinement, deprivation, danger, and technological dependence. (Perino, Bobba, & Brunelli, 1994)The role of each individual crew member can have large impacts in splitting groups and causing fissures, with personality problems scaling up from personal to intragroup. Typically, past missions have operated with a strict military inspired hierarchal structure, as difficult access to ground support means problems that need to be resolved should be dealt with on-board if necessary. These factors can be mitigated, but not prevented, through crew selection, training, and conditioning. Crew should be trained as a group under stress conditions, enhancing cohesiveness. They should also be conditioned against physical stressors like noise and odour. (Perino, Bobba, & Brunelli, 1994)

## Getting to Mars

In order to build up a colony on Mars, a means of transport would be required, of which there are currently two major viable competing ideas. This transport will have to survive the many hazards of a trip to Mars. NASA is building its SLS, Orion, and gateway system with Boeing, which is going to be used for missions to the moon as well as deep space.

### Artemis and Mars

NASA's main goal, set by Donald Trump in December 2017, is to return humans to the moon, under project Artemis. NASA intends to utilise the private sector like SpaceX and Blue Origin, for this goal (and with future missions) as part of a shift in strategy. Crucially, these plans will involve NASA investing in four main stages, a Gateway parked in lunar orbit, the SLS rocket and the Orion spacecraft, and the DST (Deep Space Transport). This strategy is, according to NASA, part of their long-term goal to send an astronaut to Mars, and will serve as a "dress rehearsal", giving NASA and other space agencies experience in deep space. The Moon also presents NASA and other space agencies the opportunity to test new tools, equipment, habitats, life support systems and to train astronauts. (NASA, 2021)

### Gateway

Building a Gateway in lunar orbit will enable months-long crew expeditions with multiple trips to the moon. However, the Gateway is also designed to operate autonomously as a deep space outpost without crew, and a base for astronauts heading to Mars. Current Gateway plans are centred around three main parts: the PPE (Power and Propulsion Element), the HALO (Habitation and Logistics Outpost), and deep space Logistics. (Dunbar, Gateway, 2022) The PPE consists of a high-power 60-kilowatt solar electric propulsion spacecraft. This will provide the gateway with power, high-rate communications, attitude control, (not altitude), and orbital transfer capabilities. (Linck, et al., 2019) HALO is the initial crew cabin for astronauts visiting the gateway, providing basic life support for visiting astronauts, thermal control, communications, environmental control and more. It will have several docking ports for visiting vehicles and future modules. In the future, ESA and JSA agreed to contribute towards the gateway's habitation, adding more modules, ESA planning the ESPRIT module, which will contain the external refuelling interface, advanced lunar communications systems, science airlock, propellant storage and more. The Canadian space agency has also agreed to provide a robotic arm like the one used around the ISS. (Dunbar, Gateway, 2022)

### SLS

![Text Box: Figure 3](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image010.png)![SLS Block 1 Crew, Block 1B Crew, Block 1B Cargo and Block ...](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image011.jpg)The SLS, or Space Launch System, is NASA's super heavy lift expendable launch vehicle, in development since 2011, when the Space Shuttle was retired. NASA intends to use the SLS to get to the moon, but also to reach Mars. Flexibility is built into the design of the SLS, with a modular system of interchangeable stages (Fig. 3). SLS Block 1, consisting of: a liquid refuelled core stage; two solid rocket boosters and four shuttle main-stage engines for the first stage; as well as a launch vehicle stage adaptor (LVSA), the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) and a crew vehicle stage adaptor (MSA). (NASA) The Solid rocket boosters, Shuttle main-engines and ICPS are based on existing technologies, and so therefore carry little risk, whilst the MSA, LVSA and Core stage are newer. SLS Block 1B will have the same core stage as Block 1, but with an interstage and exploration upper stage (replacing the ICPS), allowing it to launch more than 150t to low Earth orbit (LEO), and more than 38t to Trans Lunar Injection (TLI). Block 2 can carry 130t to LEO, and 43t to TLI with its evolved and improved boosters. (Linck, et al., 2019)

### Orion

The Orion vehicle is the Human module, also developed by Boeing, which will be NASA's vehicle for deep space travel. It will aim to carry a crew of four astronauts for up to 21 days to their destination. in the context of a Mars mission, the Orion vehicle will carry the astronauts to the DST (Deep Space Transport), and potentially dock with it on the journey to Mars. The spacecraft consists of a service module, which provides Orion with power, propulsion, thermal control, air, and water, a crew module, the habitable part of the vehicle with a heat shield capable of withstanding 2,760℃, and the launch abort system. All these factors require extensive planning, preparation, and testing, which, although necessary, is both a higher risk factor in terms of development running over time, and in terms of budget, (Linck, et al., 2019)

### DST

DST, or Deep Space Transport, is NASA's concept spacecraft for deep space missions. It will be designed for a crew of 4 to take a 1,000-day mission in deep space. It is also expected to launch on one SLS 2B cargo block, with minimal resupply and outfitting to perform once in space. Astronauts living within it will have a habitable volume of over ![](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image013.png) of space. The structure of the vehicle contains three discrete sections: a large upper layer for storage with crew quarters nested within (for radiation protection), a middle layer for research, exercise, medical, and a gallery, and finally, a central storage area in the remaining space below. The DST is designed for Mars missions, and so has to be built for 3-year missions. (Connolly, 2017) This means that many technologies used on the ISS, which tend to have around 2–3 year lifetimes, will be redundant, meaning NASA has to either evaluate and stock spares, or design new bleeding-edge technologies to replace them, both of which carry lots of risk. The DST will use a 300kW EP (Electric Propulsion) power, with a 470kW solar array, enabling it to build up speed in the near-frictionless vacuum of space. Lots of technology the DST is built on are new, posing a high risk to any missions, and causing setbacks when they inevitably fail during testing phases. This, coupled with the fact that the DST is still in concept phase, having yet to receive funding, makes that author feel that it is unlikely that the DST will be ready for a Mars mission by 2030. (Linck, et al., 2019)

## Setting up a colony on Mars

As well as getting to Mars, it is also important to discuss how humans are going to stay there. This has had little research put into it, with most energy and investment being directed at getting there in the first place, so most, if not all ideas presented below are currently conceptual. As already shown, there are many large challenges to overcome with colonising Mars, and any prospective Mars habitat will have to contend with these and establish ways around them. Though surviving at the bare minimum would be sufficient for early colonisers, in order to thrive and become self-sufficient, solutions will have to be found for generating sustainable and scalable sources of food, water, power and breathable air.

### Breathable air on Mars

Since Mars's atmosphere is so sparse, being 1% as dense as Earth's and well below the Armstrong limit, in order to maintain habitable conditions, humans must stay within pressurised environments, bringing the air pressure up from 600 pascals to 101 kilopascals, a 16,730% increase. (Vox, 2019) Furthermore, Mars's atmosphere only holds trace amounts of oxygen and nitrogen, being made up of mostly 95.9% ![](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image007.png), (Fandom, n.d.) making pressurising the habitat from Mars's atmosphere time-consuming - time that may not exist in the early stages of setting up a colony, power intensive - power that may also not exist, and expensive. Any form of colonisation will require large advances in this area, for whilst we have experience on the ISS with gaining oxygen through electrolysis (Do, Owens, Ho, Schreiner, & de Weck, 2016), the scalability of this solution for a large colony is low. Any novel solution to this problem will carry inherent risk due to inexperience lack of real-world testing, they will also require more investment to realise. This means that any early colony will inevitably have to be small, limited by the amount of atmosphere that it can sustain.

### Power on Mars

Whilst Solar power, seemingly the most obvious at first, will be crucial for any Mars colony, their efficiency or will have to be improved, for solar panels on Mars are only 40% as effective as on Earth when unobstructed by Mars's dust storms. (Nutshell, 2019) These storms can engulf the entire planet for weeks on end, fuelled by the sun's eating and the resultant convection currents within the Martian atmosphere. (Brown D. , 2014) This would drop the power output of a solar panel to near zero, with potentially catastrophic consequences for any astronauts. Another solution is nuclear technology, which will require fuel to be transported from Earth, no yielding a sustainable colony in the long term, but can be used for power in the early years of a prospective colony. NASA and the US department of energy are also studying nuclear electric and nuclear thermal propulsion for a 2 year-round trip human Mars mission. (NASA, 2021) Whilst this latter solution does not allow room for self-dependence in a Mars colony, for the first few years, it will be the most reliable option, carrying little risk given the experience in using nuclear power, the lower developmental costs, and the comparative safety.

### Food on Mars

Growing food on Mars will be a significant hurdle to jump, for the soil is toxic, holding high concentrations of chlorine and similar compounds which could be potentially fatal. (Nutshell, 2019) Despite this, by carefully adding the right nutrients, scientists at Florida and Villanova have managed to grow some crops in Earth analogues. The Villanova team found that augmenting sunlight through multi-wavelength LED's and adding potting soil helped increase their success rates, (Guinan, 2018) which the Florida group also found to be beneficial (Henly, 2016). In general, germination rates were slower than that of their Earth counterparts, though results varied across crops. Dandelions, garlic, lettuce, peas, and onions thrive, whilst plants that like autumn sun, like tomatoes, beans legumes, corn, and root plants, don't thrive very easily. (Henly, 2016) Overall, crops carry a high initial risk, for failures early on, where most likely due to inexperience, can be the most problematic, with the real chance of running out of seeds to plant. This risk will drop rapidly with each successive generation. To mitigate this, many seeds will need to be sent to Mars, possibly in a separate vehicle, to act as a backup should anything go wrong.

### Proposed Mars habitats

![Text Box: Figure 5](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image015.png)![](file:////Users/danielfussey/Library/Group%20Containers/UBF8T346G9.Office/TemporaryItems/msohtmlclip/clip_image016.png)  
There are many different proposals for a possible Mars habitat, its location, and how to deal with the unique challenges of a Mars mission listed above. To reduce the risks of negligence of key dangers, and ensure proper functioning of the colony, a schedule for each crew member, based on the schedule of an ISS crew member, is required. 8 hours of sleep. 2 hours of exercise are mandated per day, and, on EVA days, 8 hours of EVA are scheduled at a time. The most crucial factor of a Mars habitat is the ECLS (Environmental Control Life Support) system (Fig. 4). (Do, Owens, Ho, Schreiner, & de Weck, 2016) This would be largely based on the ISS's life support system, which humanity has had lots of experience with. However, this support system has never been tested in space flight or on Mars and coupled with the large number of novel technologies required to supplement the life support system for a sustainable colony to function properly, introduces some element of risk. Many of the required functions like crop growth, power, and waste disposal can be handled by a biomass plant, though the risk of supporting so many key factors with one solution yields a higher risk should the biomass plant ever fail. The Mars one mission therefore incorporates a single fault tolerance for critical life support function by including two identical life support units, redundancy that, though necessary, only adds to the 'dead' weight, and cost, of the mission. (Do, Owens, Ho, Schreiner, & de Weck, 2016) The location of a Mars habitat should ideally shield from radiation as well as storms and meteorites that can escape through Mars's comparatively thin atmosphere. Natural caves found by the Mars Odyssey in the volcano Arsia Mons have been speculated as a possible shelter for a Mars base. This triggered the Caves of Mars Project by NASA in the early 2000's, which found that "caves and subsurface constructs present the most mission effective habitat for humans", especially in the "high radiation and thermally challenging" environments of Mars. (Boston, et al., 2005) Mars has different cave types with varying levels of desirability depending on the criteria (Fig. 5). Another option are lava tubes, which, on Mars, are the remnants of basaltic lava flows associated with shield volcanoes. (Daga, et al.) Both caves and lava tubes would require sealing off, and could provide strong protection from radiation, extending Mars missions in length, as well as providing thermal insulation and cover from dust storms. On the other hand, much is uncertain about lava tubes, and there are no confirmed un-collapsed tubes. The tubes themselves may then be too small, or difficult to gain access into, and may be liable to collapse on the Martian habitat, introducing an element of danger. (Daga, et al.)

## Schedule review

The over-arching goal for NASA is for humans to set foot on the surface of Mars by 2033 (Linck, et al., 2019). The four main complex elements of NASA's timeline that will need the most extensive development and testing in coming 11 years are the SLS, Orion, Gateway, and DST. Two of these (Orion and SLS) are nearing flight readiness with a projected launch date (as of March 2022) of June 2022. Gateway is under active development, with contributions from other space agencies, speeding up the process, and thereby lowering the risk of it running overtime. Gateway's planned initial launch date is February 2024, where SpaceX will send the PPE and HALO together into orbit on a Falcon Heavy rocket, which will later be followed by more modules. (Dunbar, Gateway, 2022) The DST, though currently lacking funding, is hoped to perform a test flight in lunar vicinity by 2029 (as of 2017 predictions) (Linck, et al., 2019). This is looking increasingly in doubt because, as of 2022, no funding towards this crucial stage has come at all. Furthermore, an independent report has concluded that, due to its complexity, the DST would take 10 years of development, pushing its launch-ready date to after 2034, where NASA currently wants to be on Mars by. After this, the DST will also have to undergo several years of strenuous testing in space at the gateway and around the moon before it launches for Mars. (Linck, et al., 2019)These estimated dates are gathered based on NASA's past experiences at managing project timelines. For example, in 2010, NASA estimated that the SLS would be launch-ready by 2016, 6 years later, the SLS Block 1 is only just launch-ready, having been plagued with delays, over-ambition, budgetary concerns and failed tests. (111th Congress, the United States of America, 2010) Block 2 is estimated to be ready by 2026. Orion itself has also faced repeated delays due to various factors, dragging development of the vehicle out over 16 years since it first received finding in 2006. (NASA, 2008) The author finds that this abysmal track record sets a negative precedent for development and innovation of the Gateway and the DST, rendering and ambitious plan to colonise Mars within the next decade in increasing doubt. To surmise, the limiting factor posing greatest risk towards the scheduling of a mission to Mars is the DST, which, if ready by 2037, would mean that a mission into Mars orbit would take place at around 2039. This itself is by no means a certainty, given NASA's history with budgeting time and delays, as well as how the DST is yet to even reach approval and funding (for this projection requires development to start in 2022). These delays in all vehicles discussed (SLS, Orion, Gateway, and DST) are due to the high risk carried by their largely novel technologies. Their development must aim for total safety given their human occupants, further complicating the development process, and adding more time-expensive testing, that must not be skipped. In the author's opinion, NASA will not be able to reach Mars by its goal of 2033, and any more aggressive plan to do so will jeopardise the safety of the Astronauts, introduce more mission risk, and ultimately, set back development even further.

## Budget review

NASA, according to a report by the inspector general, (Office of Inspector General, NASA; Office of Audits, NASA, 2021) will have spent $30 billion on the SLS system by 2025. By FY 2022, NASA has already spent $22 billion on the system, which compared with the initial budgeted $7 billion is a 214% increase. Orion is also over-budget, having burnt through $21.5 billion dollars since its inception in 2006. The Gateway is projected to cost, by 2025, a total of $4.4 billion. (Office of Inspector General, NASA; Office of Audits, NASA, 2021) Alongside other factors like the human landing system, the entire Artemis program is expected by 2025 to have cost $93 billion dollars. This makes any mission to Mars look increasingly more expensive, however, development of the SLS, Gateway and Orion elements would not be included in a Mars budget, given they are already built, though they may need modifications for the longer journey, and mission costs will be lower due to the increased experience and therefore lower risk provided by missions to Mars. On the other hand, new elements are going to be required for a Mars mission that means it may follow a similar budget trajectory as Artemis, for example, the DST, which has an estimated cost of $30 billion, and a human lander, as well as any Mars base. Estimates place the cost of an orbital mission to Mars, including costs of previous test missions, at $121 billion. (Linck, et al., 2019) This budget only involves getting to Mars, but setting up a self-sustainable colony will cost, according to Elon Musk, founder of SpaceX, between $100 billion and $10 trillion. (Brown M. , 2019) Furthermore, the cost of launching each SLS, NASA's rocket for future space travel, is at the astronomical $4.1 billion (around one fifth of the entire NASA budget (Sauers, 2022)), (Office of Inspector General, NASA; Office of Audits, NASA, 2021) which, in the view of the author, is unsustainably high, severely impeding any further innovation, and stifling the rate of deep space exploration the craft is designed to provide. To surmise, the budget NASA has expended for its Artemis mission, $93 billion, can serve as a conservative analogue for the cost of a Mars mission, which most likely will cost in excess of $110 billion to get into Mars orbit. To set up a colony, though still conceptual, will cost between 12 to 14 figure sums, which, is not, in the author's view, feasible without huge amounts of support and investment.

## Moral dilemmas

Whilst solutions can be found for the technical challenges involved given enough innovation and capital, the moral questions raised by such an endeavour cannot be ignored.

### The case for colonising Mars

Before looking into the moral objections raised by a trip to Mars, it is important to first look into the motives for such journey. In a report (Tutton, 2018) that interviewed 6 of the Mars 100 (100 potential "astronauts" selected by the Mars one organisation), 4 believed that it was important to settle on Mars because doing so would "act as a catalyst for bringing people together from different regions of the world". This draws parallels with the Apollo missions being described as significant not just for Americans but for the whole of humanity, with images like "Earthrise" and "The blue marble" so widely distributed that they've been attributed to enabling a "Planetary consciousness". Other interviewees indicated that Mars was a chance to attain a new desirable social order, unifying people of all diverse backgrounds, free from the bounds of their countries, patriotism, or nationalism. One candidate raised a point that exploring is "what humans do", and that, embedded in out nature, we have had the desire to explore since we migrated out of Africa. The author feels that the interviewees in this paper all felt a conceptual affinity towards colonising Mars, that humanity should explore and inhabit Mars not necessarily because of the hard grounded facts and results of doing so - there is no guarantee that colonising Mars will unite humanity and invoke a "Planetary consciousness" - but rather because of the idea itself of becoming a multi-planetary species, as if a journey to Mars would take humanity to the next rung of development as a species. (Tutton, 2018) Another argument made for the colonisation of Mars, made by Dr Stephen Hawking, is that, in order for humanity to survive comparatively minor cosmic events, such as asteroid/cometary collision with Earth, or if we, on Earth, fail to contain climate change, trigger nuclear war, or eradicate ourselves in any other way, we need to have as second backup civilisation on another planet, a planet B. Of all the planets of out Solar system, Mars is the only real candidate for such backup colony, it lies close enough to Earth for communications to be reasonably easy and efficient, has a similar size and mass to Earth, and is rich in resources required for a young colony. (Act For Llibraries, 2017)

There is also the decidedly less altruistic argument of the resources that may be mined in abundance on the red planet, making such colony economically viable and providing incentive to do so. Whilst this is far from the utopia dreamt up by some of the Mars 100, the author finds that, based on historical evidence with the colonisation of the New World and others, that this course of action will likely be the main driving factor in expansion into Mars. (Star Base Brewery, 2022)

Finally, there is the purely research-based colonisation of Mars, one where the only purpose of humans on Mars is to study Mars and other experiments that may require it. This scientific approach is, in the authors opinion, the main driving factor behind the current Mars missions nearing viability and is what will and is driving initial exploration towards Mars. In summary, the author feels that scientific exploration will be the main driving factor towards an initial colony, but if and after any precious metals are found, greed will eventually fuel growth, before the colony citizens start to demand a nicer world, and the colony's economy becomes self-sufficient on itself.

### The case against colonising Mars

An often-cited issue with colonising Mars is contamination, that humans would bring with them trillions of microbes. For context, NASA's Perseverance rover was allowed to have just 41,000 spores. There is the real risk that any spores or microbes that manage to make their way onto the Martian surface would irreversibly confuse the search for Martian life, one of the key reasons, as stated above, for early Mars missions. Furthermore, if life does exist, these invasive microbes could outcompete any native life. From an ethical standpoint, this violates the principle of scientific conservation, whereby destruction of an object for scientific interest is only morally permissible in certain scenarios including when no adequate alternatives are available. With Mars and the search for life on Mars, they are adequate alternatives available, namely robots like the rovers that already wander the surface of Mars, therefore, bringing in destruction in the form of invasive microbes would explicitly violate this principle. Whist some believe that it would be possible to protect Mars form microbial contamination, the scientific consensus indicates that this would not be possible, or that, regardless of possibility, the risk is simply not worth the reward. (Bharmal, 2018)

A strong counterargument to the human 'arc' that Mars might serve as is that Earth-based bunkers are cheaper, more effective, and can hold more people. Furthermore, in the event of such mass-extinction event, chances are that the Earth that emerges will still be orders of magnitude more habitable than Mars, which is a cold, barren, radiation ridden wasteland with the tendency towards planet-wide dust storms, an inhospitable atmosphere, and such low pressure as to make all bodily fluids boil instantly. (Bharmal, 2018)

Furthermore, there is little in the way of scientific exploration that cannot be done by the rovers we already use, or that warrant an entire colony’s worth of explorers, especially when the scientists are likely to accidentally destroy what they are looking for through the trillions of microbes they will bring.

Perhaps the most extensively used argument is the belief that we should fix Earth first, and that the $121 billion NASA is spending to get to Mars and the potential trillions estimated for a self-sufficient Mars colony is money better spent on Earth. Furthermore, the brains set on tackling the challenge of getting to Mars would be better used on real-world issues on Earth, such as addressing climate change. Before seeking the next step for the human race, it is argued that it would be better to first fix ourselves and help bring those of us lagging behind in the previous step up. Earth has no shortage of problems, from its ocean filled with plastic to its rife social inequality, but Earth is still nonetheless overwhelmingly more habitable and desirable, with everything required to sustain life, and allow it to thrive.  

# Conclusions

There are many questions thrown up as to whether a journey to Mars, and its subsequent colonisation, is viable. Mars is a hostile, frigid, world laced with pernicious radiation and both a toxic atmosphere and soil. In order to get there in the best rockets we have, requires upwards of $121 billion, decades of development, using valuable resources and minds, as well as huge political, and therefore public, support, and to stay there requires up to $10 trillion, constant expensive rocket trips (at around $4.1 billion each) and a habitat capable of keeping colonisers safe, secure, and comfortable. This means that any reason for going to Mars will have to justify the journey beyond the intangible conceptual feeling that humanity ought to be multi-planetary, that staying on Mars will kick-start a new space era, as seen in science-fiction fantasies. Yes, Mars captures the public’s imagination, but if the intention is to spend the equivalent to around half the GDP of the United States in 2018 attaining it, there will have to be a strong reason to convince the likely inimical public and media.

Firstly, the author believes that the technological viability of this mission is largely sound, carrying low risk. This is because the technology, though new, has, and will be tested thoroughly. The risk will significantly drop later in the process of colonisation as the technology matures, further improving viability. The largest challenges, and greatest barriers towards complete viability in this area are the DST component of getting to Mars, and the eventual habitat and ECLS, all of which have received little to no funding and development.

Secondly, the only way the author finds that such a mission within the next two decades could become economically viable would be on the discovery of resources on Mars, but even then, a Mars colony is likely to cost the combined value of the world’s 10 highest ranked public companies, which shows the slim chance of any profit. Unless other external factors arise to allow such large amounts of money to be spent, for example how the cold war was an enabler for the Apollo missions, which would otherwise not have happened so quickly, so successfully, a Mars colonisation mission is unlikely to arise. This is because current rocket prices are unsustainably high, but, as time passes and the technology matures and experience with it improves, the economic viability of such will drop to acceptable levels.

Finally, the author believes there are still many moral questions to be answered and that these questions should ideally be answered by humanity as a whole, though this is far from likely in reality. The view the author takes is that the only moral way to justify colonisation and expansion into another planet is when the economic viability drops down to levels where no excessive spending is necessary, for Earth is by far a preferable location, with everything life requires in plenty. But this economic viability must not be found from the exploitation of Mars’s natural resources, which therefore drops the viability of a Mars colony within the next two decades. Given the assumption that humanity will eventually colonise Mars, the risks of contamination towards any possible life on Mars would have to be accepted as an inevitable consequence, and that mitigation is unlikely to be that effective. Consequently, either any successful search for Martian life must occur before widespread contamination, or it should be assumed that Mars is void of life unless proven otherwise, for you cannot commit moral crimes against something that does not exist.

To conclude, the technical side of a mission to colonise Mars in the next two decades will eventually be viable, though this will not be by NASA’s plan of the 2030’s due to delays and uncertainty about all the novel technology, and so will have to happen within the 2nd decade. However, the pace of technological innovation is intrinsically tied towards how much money is fed into it. This economic viability the author finds to be potentially unviable, based purely off the large sums of money required for doing so. Whilst this amount of money is available, and significant portions of it are already being spent, it remains largely in the domain of governments. Consequentially in order for a government to justify spending such sums, there must be a valid, tangible purpose for doing so, as opposed to the conceptual ideals of such a mission. The void of convincing justification for a mission for colonisation and the enormous amounts of capital required do not balance out. Nonetheless, even if such capital and purpose could be found, is it even possible to justify the ethical dichotomy of an extravagant journey to Mars and the disasters being unleashed onto our own planet? How is it possible to justify spending one eighth of the total global economy on Mars whilst the humans left on Earth still suffer from plethoric inequalities? Regardless of where you stand on this subjective argument, the author believes that there will inevitably be a significant amount of people arguing on either side. This consequentially means that the full public support required for a government to do such a move would not be present, so in turn the economic viability would plummet, for the capital required would not materialise, and so the technical viability within the next two decades would drop, as innovation stalls due to the lack of funds. It is for this reason that the author finds a mission to colonise Mars to be unviable.
