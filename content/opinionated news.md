# the problem with opinionated news
It took 22 days for the press to drive a Jason Arday to suicide:
After 13 days, and under the weight of 41 news articles, he resigned.
After a further **9 days**, under the weight of **188** **new** hit-pieces, he ended his life. 
**21 hit pieces a day till he died.**

What was the story?

A story about plagiarism at top universities does not justify the 560% increase in personal attacks **after** the resignation.

## 1 - the motivation
#### ideology?

## 2 - the convenient truth
News outlets like *The Daily Telegraph* or *The Guardian* can *often* ***correctly*** claim to treat *factual news* impartially, in part because UK law requires them to do so, and in part because this is of vital importance to a readership who turn to their pages with a desire to learn the truth about the world. 

*So what's the issue?*

Whilst the reports may be presented impartially, the **choice** of which stories to cover are most certainly **not**. Where the Guardian might choose a story about climate change or homelessness, the Telegraph might instead focus on the economy or immigration. By feeding their readership news that panders to an ideology, combined with a robust opinion section devoid of balance, a paper can effectively present a vision of the world beleaguered by the convenient problems and devoid of the inconvenient truths.

## 3 - ships in the night 
### split picture
The discerning Telegraph reader who might venture a peek at the Guardian for balance will often be presented not with a counterargument on an opinion they've recently read, but rather another persuasive opinion on an entirely different issue. A reader is not pulled towards two different views but rather twisted across different problems. It is not possible to resolve a debate between two news sources when one asks how to fund public services and the other how to deregulate the market. Media framing tells you not what to think but rather what to think about.
### "full picture"
Where reporting does overlap, reading different viewpoints only surfaces the benefits of each - rarely, if ever, providing any counterargument. It is simply insufficient to appraise one opinion by reading the benefits of an opposing one, doing so leaves both viewpoints unopposed, a mirage of rebuttal in a desert of separate advocacy. 

When presented with these two unopposed viewpoints, how does a reader form their opinion?

An exchange of ideas is reduced simply to a battle of wits, devolved to the point where rhetoric trumps refutation. Two ships sailing in different directions on the same sea, yet without the necessary confrontation that crowns a victor.
